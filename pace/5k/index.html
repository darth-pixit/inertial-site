Pace

5K pace charts

A 5K pace chart answers one question: what pace, in min/km and min/mi, finishes 5 km (3.11 mi) in a time you can name. The pages below are the times people actually look up, from 20:00 to 35:00. Each one prints the even pace, the kilometre splits, the mile splits, and the clock time at halfway.

How to read a 5K pace chart

Start with the finish time, not the pace. Pick the chart whose time is the one you are willing to see on the clock, then read the pace that produces it if you hold it the whole way. A 20:00 5K is 4:00/km, which is 6:26/mi. A 35:00 5K is 7:00/km. The charts between those two do the same division for 22:30, 25:00 and 30:00, so you do not have to.

Halfway on a 5K is 2.5 km (1.55 mi). On even pace the clock there is exactly half the finish time: 10:00 in a 20:00 race, 12:30 in a 25:00 race, 17:30 in a 35:00 race. That is the number to glance at. If you are well ahead of it, you started too fast for the time on the chart. If you are a few seconds behind, you are still in the race the chart describes.

Min/km and min/mi

Every chart gives both units, metric first. The mile pace is the kilometre pace multiplied by 1.609344, which is why 4:00/km is 6:26/mi and not a number you get by doubling. If your watch is set to min/mi and you pace off the min/km column, you will run the wrong race. Check the unit on the screen before the start, then use the matching column.

The splits are the same pace added up. The last row is the finish, not a rounded kilometre. Five kilometres is five rows in the kilometre table. The mile table ends at 3.11 mi, so the last row is a partial mile. That partial row is the one that makes the elapsed time equal the finish time on the chart.

The first kilometre

A 5K is short enough that the opening kilometre decides the day. Run the first kilometre 10 seconds slower than the chart and you have given away 10 seconds. Run every kilometre 10 seconds slower and you finish 50 seconds late, because 10 seconds times 5 km is 50 seconds. There is no long second half in which to "find it". The chart pace has to be the pace at kilometre 1, when it feels easy and the people around you are going faster.

Even pace is the plan. A negative split on a 5K is a few seconds, not a minute. Going through 2.5 km faster than the halfway clock is how a 25:00 attempt becomes 26:00. The negative split page says what the phrase means. On this distance, treat it as a small cushion, not a strategy that lets you bank time early.

Watch distance and the GPS trace

Certified courses are measured on the shortest legal route. You will not run that line, and a GPS watch adds a little more distance because each point sits slightly off the true position. Together that is often about 1 to 1.5% extra on the watch. On a 20:00 5K, 1.5% is 18 seconds. The chart's "watch pace" row is the slightly faster number the screen has to show if the trace runs long and you still want the clock time in the title. The full idea is the same one on the pace calculator, and the GPS accuracy entry says why the trace grows.

If your goal is not one of these five times, do not interpolate in your head. The calculator does any 5K time with the same splits and the same halfway maths. The 10K pace charts are the next distance up, and they are a different effort: a pace you can hold for 10 km (6.21 mi), not for 5.
