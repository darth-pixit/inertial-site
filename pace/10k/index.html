Pace

10K pace charts

A 10K pace chart turns a finish time into a pace you can hold for 10 km (6.21 mi). The charts here cover 40:00, 45:00, 50:00, 55:00 and 60:00. Each page has the even pace in min/km and min/mi, the splits in both units, the halfway clock time, and the pace to put on a watch that measures the course a little long.

How to use these charts

Choose the finish time first. The pace on the page is what that time requires if every kilometre costs the same. A 40:00 10K is 4:00/km (6:26/mi), the same pace as a 20:00 5K held for twice the distance. A 60:00 10K is 6:00/km (9:39/mi). The three charts in between are the same division at 45:00, 50:00 and 55:00.

Halfway is 5 km (3.11 mi). On even pace the clock reads half the finish time: 20:00 in a 40:00 race, 25:00 in a 50:00 race, 30:00 in a 60:00 race. That 5 km mark is the check. Ahead of it by much, and you are running a faster chart than the one you opened. A few seconds behind is still the chart you chose.

Min/km, min/mi, and the last split

Read the column that matches the watch. Min/mi is min/km times 1.609344, so the two columns are the same pace in two unit systems, not two different plans. The kilometre table has ten full rows only if you ignore the fact that some watches tick a partial at the end. The mile table runs to 6.21 mi, so the last row is a partial mile whose split is shorter than the others. The elapsed time on that last row is the finish time. If a chart you find elsewhere ends on a round mile and a round kilometre that do not add up to 10 km, it has rounded the distance away.

Where 10K efforts fail

The pace feels easy at 2 km and stops feeling easy around 7 km. That is the shape of this distance. Most people who miss the time on the chart do it by running the first 3 km (1.86 mi) at 5K effort. A 5K pace is a different page: the 5K pace charts are for 5 km, and borrowing one for the opening of a 10K spends the second half.

Ten seconds a kilometre slower than the chart, held the whole way, is 100 seconds late, because 10 seconds times 10 km is 100 seconds. There is no mystery in that. The chart is the plan. A small negative split — a few seconds slower through 5 km than the halfway clock — is the version that still finishes near the title. Banking a minute in the first half is not. The negative split entry is the definition. On a 10K it is a small adjustment, not a licence to go out hard.

The watch will read long

A GPS trace of a 10K is often about 1 to 1.5% longer than the measured course, for the same reason as any other race: you do not run the shortest line, and the points themselves wobble. On a 40:00 10K, 1.5% is 36 seconds. The watch-pace line on each chart is the number that still produces the title time when the trace is long. How the arithmetic is done is on the pace calculator, and why the distance grows is on GPS accuracy.

If your time is not in this set, use the calculator rather than splitting the difference between two charts. The half marathon pace charts are the next step up, over 21.0975 km (13.11 mi), and the pace that feels right at 7 km of a 10K is not automatically the pace for a half.
