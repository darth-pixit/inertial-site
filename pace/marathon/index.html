Pace

Marathon pace charts

A marathon pace chart answers "what pace is that finish time?" for 42.195 km (26.22 mi). The charts here are the times people search for: 3:00:00, 3:30:00, 3:45:00, 4:00:00, 4:15:00, 4:30:00, 5:00:00 and 5:30:00. Each page is one time, with the even pace in min/km and min/mi, the splits, the halfway clock, and the pace a watch has to show when its trace runs long.

How to use a marathon pace chart

Pick the finish time you are actually preparing, and read its page. The pace is the division of that time by 42.195 km. A 4:00:00 marathon is 5:41/km (9:09/mi). A 3:00:00 marathon is 4:16/km. The other six charts are the same division at their own times. Do not average two neighbouring charts in your head if your goal sits between them. The pace calculator does the in-between time with the same splits.

Halfway is 21.0975 km (13.11 mi). On even pace the clock reads half the finish time: 1:30:00 in a 3:00:00 marathon, 2:00:00 in a 4:00:00 marathon, 2:45:00 in a 5:30:00 marathon. Write that number down. It is the only split that matters if you remember nothing else on the page. The half marathon pace charts describe a race that ends at this point. A marathon is the same clock time again, on tired legs, which is why the halfway check is the whole plan.

Even pace, and a small negative split

Even pace means every kilometre costs the same. A 2% negative split, which each chart also prints, puts you through halfway a little slower than even and brings you home a little faster. On a 4:00:00 marathon, even halfway is 2:00:00. The slower halfway on that page is the 2% version. Arriving at halfway faster than even is not "banked time". It is time you will repay after 30 km (18.6 mi), usually at a worse rate than you saved it.

Ten seconds a kilometre slower than the chart, held for 42.195 km, is about 7 minutes late. That is arithmetic: 10 seconds times 42.195 km. It is also why the first hour is where marathons are lost. At 10 km (6.21 mi) the chart pace has to feel conversational. If it already feels like a 10K, the second half of the chart will not happen. Men are more likely than women to slow in the marathon — that is a finding about marathon pacing specifically, from Deaner and colleagues in 2015, and it is why this sentence lives on the marathon hub and not on a 5K chart.

Whether the time itself is realistic is a different question from the pace. The pace page will tell you 5:41/km for four hours even if your training does not support four hours. The sub-4 reality check is the page for that, and the race predictor puts three published models next to each other so you can see the spread.

When the watch measures the course long

A certified marathon is measured on the shortest legal route, with a short-course prevention factor so the course is not accidentally short. You will run wide on corners, around people, and away from the tangent. A GPS watch then adds a bit more, because each recorded point is slightly off the path and the errors accumulate. The trace is often 1 to 1.5% longer than the distance you were scored on.

On a 4:00:00 marathon, pacing the watch as if 1.5% of extra distance were not there means the screen has to show about 5:36/km (9:01/mi), not 5:41/km (9:09/mi), if you still want 4:00:00 on the clock. Each chart prints that watch pace for its own time. The calculator does it for a time that is not listed. The reason the distance grows is written up under GPS accuracy. The habit of running the first half faster than even, which makes the watch problem worse, is the negative split page read the other way round: even, planned; only slightly negative, executed.

Heat is not in the pace. A warm day, or a high dew point, can make the chart pace the wrong goal even though the division is still right. Change the goal time, then come back to the chart. The chart will not change itself.
